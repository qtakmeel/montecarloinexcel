Will your savings last? Simulate 500 retirements in Excel.

Average returns are a mirage. The order in which they arrive can be the difference between a comfortable thirty years and running out at year nineteen. A Monte Carlo simulation in Excel shows you the whole distribution of futures, not just the average one.

~11 min read · every formula shown · works in Excel 2010+, LibreOffice Calc, Google Sheets

The average is not the answer

A portfolio returning 7% per year on average does not return 7% per year. It returns +30% some years and −18% others, and the order of those returns matters enormously once you are withdrawing money. Retire into a crash and every early withdrawal sells assets at the bottom, permanently scarring the portfolio. Retire into a boom and the same average return produces a very different life. This is sequence-of-returns risk, and no average, however precisely quoted, captures it.

What captures it is running the retirement hundreds of times with different random return sequences and counting how many survive. That is a Monte Carlo simulation, and it needs exactly one random formula and one compounding formula.

Step 1: The inputs you must choose honestly

  • Starting balance — what is actually in the account today.
  • Annual withdrawal — what you plan to take out each year, in dollars, adjusted for nothing (keep it simple first).
  • Mean return — the arithmetic mean annual return of your assumed allocation. For a typical stock-heavy portfolio, historical US arithmetic means land around 8–10%; pick your own number and defend it.
  • Standard deviation — the annual volatility of that allocation, historically around 15–18% for the same kind of portfolio.

Note the word arithmetic. The geometric mean (what you actually compound at, with no cash flows) is lower than the arithmetic mean by roughly half the variance squared. Feeding a geometric mean into a simulation that already models volatility double-counts the drag and understates risk.

Step 2: One random return per year

Model each year's return as a normal random variable:

=NORMINV(RAND(), $B$6, $B$7)

RAND() produces a uniform number between 0 and 1; NORMINV converts it into a draw from the normal distribution with your chosen mean and standard deviation. A draw of −0.18 is a −18% year. That is the entire randomness engine.

Step 3: The compounding loop, one row per year

Down 30 rows (one per retirement year), the balance evolves as:

=(previous_balance + withdrawal) * (1 + this_years_return)

with the first row anchored to your starting balance. The withdrawal is added before growth because the money comes out at the start of the year; flip the order if you prefer end-of-year withdrawals, and you will see it matters, which is itself a lesson.

Watch for the failure mode this formula makes visible: a few bad early years and the balance goes negative. That is not a bug. That is the retirement that didn't work, and the simulation exists to count them.

Step 4: Run 500 retirements

Copy the 30-year block across 500 columns (or stack 500 blocks), each an independent future. At the end of each, record the final balance:

Success: =IF(final_balance >= 0, 1, 0)
Success rate: =SUM(success_column)/500
Median outcome: =MEDIAN(finals)

Every press of F9 re-runs all 15,000 years of all 500 retirements. What you are looking at is not a forecast. It is the shape of the risk you are actually carrying.

The 92% is the number that matters, and it is also the number the average-return calculation would have hidden from you entirely: compounding the mean return for 30 years shows a comfortable surplus, with no mention of the 8% of futures that end at zero.

Step 6: Use it the way professionals do

  • Stress the withdrawal. Raise the annual withdrawal by 10% and watch the success rate fall. The sensitivity tells you how much slack you really have.
  • Stress the sequence. Lower the mean by 1% (a worse decade than expected) and re-run. This is the honest version of a "conservative assumption".
  • Connect to the 4% rule. The famous rule of thumb is itself the output of a historical simulation. Your spreadsheet lets you test it against your own numbers instead of inheriting someone else's.

What this model gets wrong, on purpose

Annual normal returns are a simplification. Real markets have fat tails (crashes bigger than a normal distribution expects), returns cluster (volatile years come in groups), and inflation eats the withdrawal's purchasing power. Each can be added to this spreadsheet in a line or two, and adding them yourself is precisely how you learn what they cost. Start simple, see the shape of the risk, then complicate.

This article is education, not financial advice, and the spreadsheet is a model, not an oracle. The point is to replace one comforting number with an honest distribution.

The whole thing in five sentences

  1. Choose a starting balance, a withdrawal, a mean return and a standard deviation.
  2. Draw each year's return with NORMINV(RAND(), mean, sd).
  3. Compound balance minus withdrawal for 30 years; flag any future that hits zero.
  4. Run 500 independent futures and count survivors.
  5. Stress the inputs until you know which one the plan is secretly afraid of.
